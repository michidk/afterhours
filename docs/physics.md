# Physics decision

Retain the small custom planar movement/contact solver for the current 16-player foundation. Rapier 3D is a runtime dependency for debris and corpses; vehicle movement still uses the custom solver. This is a scoped release decision, not a claim that a custom solver is better than Rapier.

The reproducible contact-only benchmark (`bun run bench:physics`, [raw results](physics-benchmark.json)) compares 120 equal rectangle bodies at 120 Hz: three custom SAT passes/grid queries against Rapier's default solver with CCD. After 120 warmup steps, 599 samples measured custom p95 **4.97 ms** versus Rapier **0.33 ms** on Node 24 / Windows. Rapier also stopped a 600 m/s body at a thin wall. Neither benchmark includes tyre forces, AI, snapshots, vertical filtering, or all gameplay costs. Engine implementations have different solver behaviour; this is not a strict quality comparison.

Rapier therefore offers a clear performance/contact robustness advantage for denser worlds. Its body forces and ray/shape queries could support the requested wheel model, and its WebAssembly compatibility package runs in Node and browsers. See official [simulation structures](https://rapier.rs/docs/user_guides/javascript/simulation_structures/), [CCD](https://rapier.rs/docs/user_guides/javascript/rigid_body_ccd/), and [determinism constraints](https://rapier.rs/docs/user_guides/javascript/determinism/).

For this release the custom approach already provides shared prediction-safe movement and server-controlled altitude/clearance, and the full city has met its 33.33 ms CPU tick budget at 16 clients. Migrating both authority and prediction requires explicit world reconstruction/rollback, collision height groups, contact-damage hooks, footprint queries, and matching tyre behaviour. Do that before raising the cap or adding stacking/rigid-body destruction, rather than spending the measured headroom by increasing density. Regression coverage does not prove arbitrary continuous collision safety: substeps and speed limits cover this release's vehicle speeds and footprint sizes.

## Vehicle model

The four-contact tyre model follows the mechanics in [Pat Kerr's 2D vehicles](https://patkerr.co.uk/2d-vehicles/#recreation-note) and its linked Motion Lab source: wheel point velocity includes yaw, front wheel steering rotates the lateral basis, wheel forces generate both linear acceleration and torque, engine power drives the body, rolling resistance opposes wheel-forward motion, and rear handbrake grip loss recovers over 150 ms. Rear slip thresholds have hysteresis and produce wheel-position tyre marks. Cars have mass/inertia; angular collision impulses respond to off-centre impacts.

This is an independent TypeScript implementation with attribution, not copied source. The sedan matches the reference force constants (1000 kg, 9750 N engine, 1100 lateral coefficient and 32.5 rolling coefficient per wheel). Vehicle variants and arcade speed limits are game-specific tuning. Fixed force tests verify rolling decay and steering torque; regression tests cover grip recovery, lateral handbrake behaviour, off-centre contact response, walls, jumps, damage and replay. It does not model suspension or a full friction-circle tyre simulation.

## Ramps, prediction and tyre feedback

`shared/physics/ramps.ts` defines each existing artifact ramp as a 5 × 7 m wedge rising 1.6 m toward positive z. Rendering uses those same dimensions. Cars entering at the toe follow the surface at any speed with tyre forces active; the lip preserves uphill vertical velocity before gravity takes over. Low side/back approaches collide with the solid section above their height, and descending cars can land on the slope. This replaces the former invisible centre trigger requiring more than 12 m/s, which ordinary 8 m/s AI traffic could never activate. Altitude remains an arcade height layer, without suspension or rigid-body pitch dynamics.

Client prediction solves static and dynamic contacts at the same 120 Hz substeps with the shared finite-mass/angular impulse solver. Nearby snapshot bodies are cloned into a private prediction world and carried across pending inputs; authoritative acknowledgements reconstruct that world and replay only remaining inputs. Parked cars use shared tyre damping, while other controlled bodies coast using their last known velocities. Their future inputs and contact outcomes remain unknown, so corrections are expected. Distant remote rendering retains its interpolation buffer; nearby bodies render from the same private contact world as the player. See [networking bounds and research](networking.md). Correction smoothing includes shortest-arc yaw and constrains the displayed controlled body against obstacles; render extrapolation never changes replay state or wheel-slip arrays. Final damage, health, knockdowns and occupancy come only from snapshots.

Tyre trails are cosmetic and generated from displayed wheel positions/slip on every client. Local predicted slip produces rubber immediately; remote tracks follow interpolated cars rather than arriving ahead of their displayed tyres. Distance sampling stitches segments and resets across airborne movement, missing entities and large position discontinuities. New dirty texture tiles upload that frame; fading updates every 200 ms. The existing 1,800-mark/40-second caps remain. No server skid events are sent, eliminating both network delay and duplicate authoritative echoes. Clients can draw slightly different trails, and a newly joined client starts without historical marks.
